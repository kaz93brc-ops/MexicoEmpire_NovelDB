## Summary

Hamnett frames the late Juárez administration as strengthened by the conflicts of 1867-1872 but exposed by them: Juárez relied on federal authority, army officers, state governments, jefes políticos, and local caciques because he could not command the Liberal Party itself. Puebla, Guerrero, and Oaxaca show central power working through governors, generals, National Guard contingents, and sierra caciques. On p.205 Hamnett shifts to Ignacio Mejía as the key late-administration figure, emphasizing army loyalty, commander rivalry, demobilization plans, persistent troop levels, military spending, and budget deficits. The page then opens, but does not complete, a church-policy point about a discreet modus vivendi.

## Creative Use

### Historical Use

- Use post-1867 Juárez as a ruler struggling with provincial rebellion, state factions, army loyalty, and military spending.
- Use Mejía as a central military-political figure distinct from Lerdo.
- Use Puebla, Guerrero, and Oaxaca as contrasting examples of central power mediated through governors, generals, National Guard contingents, and caciques.
- Use military spending and stalled demobilization to show the continued structure of a war state after imperial collapse.
- Use the church modus vivendi opening to avoid flattening Juárez into a simply anti-religious figure.

### Creative Inference

- Juárez choosing bureaucracy, state governments, jefes políticos, and the army over direct party control.
- Mejía watching Escobedo, Díaz, and Corona compete while preserving army loyalty.
- Oaxaca sierra caciques weighing central support against local advantage.
- Congressional opponents turning provincial conflict into a charge of centralism and executive abuse.
- Juárez exploring a church compromise quietly rather than as public ideological reversal.
