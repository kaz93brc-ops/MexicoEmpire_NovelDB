## Summary

Hamnett closes the chapter by rejecting a single-cause U.S.-pressure explanation for French withdrawal. He treats U.S. pressure as part of a larger French problem that included military position, domestic opposition, and growing European danger.

The page then turns to the limits of U.S. republican solidarity. Iglesias's reaction to Seward's 1867 plea for Maximilian's life frames U.S. pressure as an arrogant State Department demand, while Hamnett reads U.S. policy as European balance-of-power diplomacy that prioritized France over Mexico.

Hamnett distinguishes Romero's and Juárez's hopes from practical outcomes. Romero lost early hopes in Seward's policy, Juárez kept some long-term faith in U.S. possibilities but saw short-term expectations fail, and Mexican distrust of U.S. intentions remained latent until it revived after 1898-99.

Romero's July 1868 Washington farewell statement praised the United States as the first great power to treat Mexico as an equal. Hamnett qualifies that claim by emphasizing U.S. perceptions of Mexican inferiority and the larger asymmetry between a declining nineteenth-century Mexico and a rising continental United States.

p.163 opens the notes section. Visible notes 1-3 are Chapter 7 source-trail bibliography, not notes 31-33, and should not be confused with the conclusion's unresolved note references.

## Creative Use

### Historical Use

- Iglesias reads Seward's plea as foreign overreach rather than neutral humanitarianism.
- Romero praises the United States in Washington while the reader knows the relationship is unequal.
- Juárez keeps a constitutional future vision while accepting that immediate U.S. support has disappointed him.
- Mexican and U.S. diplomats discuss an unavoidable relationship that is neither friendship nor open enmity.
- French withdrawal is debated through U.S. pressure, French military vulnerability, domestic opposition, and European crisis.

### Creative Inference

- Iglesias drafts a controlled but angry response to a State Department demand.
- Romero performs optimism in Washington while privately remembering Seward's caution.
- Juárez speaks of shared republican principles while fearing Mexican subordination.
- Seward avoids provoking Napoleon III while keeping U.S. moral language available.
- Maximilian's side interprets foreign clemency as civilized pressure while republicans hear interference.
