## Summary

p.156 resolves the Lincoln arms-export continuation as 2 November 1862 and Hamnett interprets the ban as sharply weakening the republic's resistance capacity when the French resumed their advance. Juárez opposed Confederate agents and the Confederacy while Mexico's formal Civil War policy remained neutral.

The page then treats the Matamoros-Brownsville cotton and arms trade, Union seizure of Brownsville in November 1863, the shift of Confederate trade toward Piedras Negras and Nuevo Laredo, and Seward's wish to avoid a pretext for armed conflict with France. As French forces moved toward the Valley of Mexico, Juárez withdrew to San Luis Potosí, while Lincoln opposed the Intervention and monarchy project but recalled Corwin.

Seward's 10 September 1863 message to the British minister, his neutrality instructions, Confederate approaches to Napoleon III, and Napoleon III's refusal to recognize the Confederacy all show U.S., French, imperial, and Confederate diplomacy crossing without simple pro- or anti-Juárez alignment. p.157 adds Romero's Washington political work, the 17-month gap without a resident U.S. minister, Juárez's Franklin-Washington-New York communication routes, and Hamnett's account of Juárez expecting Northern victory while recognizing both U.S. power and lack of direct support.

The page closes by introducing Lerdo's strengthened foreign-policy influence during the wandering-cabinet period, his nationalist opposition to humiliating foreign bargains, the Wyke-Zamacona precedent, and his McLane-Ocampo caution. The quoted warning continues onto the next page.
