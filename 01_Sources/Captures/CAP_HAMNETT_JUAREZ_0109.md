## Summary

pp.212-213 continue the Guerrero civil-war section by showing Jiménez supporters accusing Juárez of tolerating Álvarez family dominance. Hamnett distinguishes Diego Álvarez from his father Juan by presenting Diego as less politically capable and less able to preserve Juan's broad popular base. Diego held out at Hacienda La Providencia with armed followers and asked Juárez to act against the Jiménez faction.

Ignacio Altamirano appears as a Liberal intellectual voice sympathetic to Jiménez and critical of Álvarez family power as a local, household-based authority needing republican correction. Álvarez's side answered by accusing Altamirano of fomenting anarchy. Hamnett then moves from discourse to armed politics: the 1867 elections fixed armed factions into local zones of control, Jiménez rejected Juárez's appeal for restraint, and Juárez sent the outsider General Francisco Arce to mediate and pacify.

Juárez tried in an 1868-04-22 letter to persuade Álvarez to withdraw voluntarily from the governorship and go to Mexico City under special federal permission, framing this as a personal proposal rather than an executive command. Arce ordered Álvarez to dissolve his private force. Álvarez refused, arguing that withdrawal would look like an admission of wrongdoing and that leaving La Providencia would expose him to murder by Jiménez supporters.

Arce reported Álvarez's strong Pacific-coast authority, while Álvarez protested Arce's small garrisons at Acapulco and Tlapa, especially the implied threat to Acapulco customs revenue. By 1870 the Jiménez faction was gaining National Guard support in Tixtla and Chilapa. The Guerrero legislature attacked Arce as unconstitutional, and by June 1870 Arce was forced out while the Tixtla state government fell to the Jiménez faction.

In October 1870 Juárez asked Álvarez to lead federal forces to restore Arce. Hamnett treats this as evidence that juarismo in Guerrero depended on the Álvarez network. The federal government, short of funds, allowed Acapulco customs revenue to be diverted to pay Álvarez's three coastal battalions and promised more support. Arce recovered Tixtla, but Hamnett stresses that the Jiménez faction's temporary collapse did not end Guerrero disorder; it fed into anti-Juárez porfirista opposition. The final sentence begins Jiménez's La Noria action but remains unfinished.
