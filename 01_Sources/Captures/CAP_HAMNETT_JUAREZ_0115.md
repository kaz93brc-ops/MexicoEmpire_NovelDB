## Summary

p.224 completes the previous spread's unfinished sentence by turning from El Chato's armed force and capitation-tax support to the National Guard. Hamnett presents the National Guard as a Liberal federalist institution that Juárez himself had used in Oaxaca, but which after 1867 became the military base of anti-Juárez local Liberal power under Félix Díaz. Hamnett compares Félix Díaz's Oaxaca position to Santiago Vidaurri's north-eastern power in 1855-64 and treats it as both humiliating and threatening to the state government.

The page then follows Félix Díaz's 1871 position: he was re-elected in Oaxaca on 25 June 1871, refused Juárez in the July presidential election, and supported Porfirio Díaz. Oaxaca's sixteen electoral districts are said to have voted for Porfirio Díaz. The Juchitán / Isthmus material reopens the earlier Juárez governorship problem: resistance to political control and taxation revived in 1870, Albino Jiménez attacked state militia, and unrest spread toward Ixtaltepec and Petapa. Díaz sent the Guerrero National Guard Battalion but failed to subdue the Juchitecos, then went to the Isthmus himself after 15 December 1871.

p.224-p.225 summarize the violent Juchitán repression, including 27-29 December resistance, a bayonet assault, pursuit of Juchitecos into villages and forests, execution of a parish priest, and removal and mutilation of a St Vincent Ferrer statue. These details are kept as probable or uncertain and verification-needed, especially where they appear to depend on testimony or later source notes. Hamnett interprets the repression and sacred-image destruction as producing revenge and resentment that weakened Félix Díaz beneath his outward strength.

p.225 begins the new La Noria section. Porfirio Díaz prepared another challenge to Juárez in the July 1871 presidential election; his Hacienda of La Noria became a center of political plotting. Lerdo wanted to succeed Juárez but moved into opposition when Juárez sought re-election, allowing lerdistas and porfiristas in Congress to form a tactical anti-Juárez alliance. Juárez won the largest vote but no absolute majority, and Congress declared him constitutionally elected on 12 October 1871. Hamnett then has Díaz abandon legal victory and turn to La Noria rebellion. Oaxaca became the rebellion's base because El Chato had won the governorship; the Oaxaca state government published the Plan of La Noria on 8 November 1871 and initially framed its action as suspending recognition of the federal government and restoring state sovereignty.
