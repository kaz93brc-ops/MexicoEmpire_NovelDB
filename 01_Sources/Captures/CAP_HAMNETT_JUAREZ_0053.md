## Summary

p.100 completes the prior page's constitutional-oath sentence: the archbishop's line made absolution conditional on public retraction of the oath and notice to civil authorities. Hamnett judges this policy as placing ordinary Catholics in a severe dilemma, enraging Liberals, and compounding an already damaging government error.

The new Oaxaca section turns to Bishop José Agustín Domínguez and Governor Juárez. Domínguez, though criticized by Conservatives, performed a Te Deum at Oaxaca Cathedral on 1856-01-10 to welcome Juárez back to the state. On 1857-03-23 Juárez put the federal Constitution into effect in Oaxaca, and a second publicly funded Te Deum solemnized the constitutional oath despite Domínguez's objections to several constitutional articles.

The third Te Deum, intended to celebrate Juárez's constitutional election as governor on 1857-06-27, did not occur. Cathedral clergy and canons refused, with opposition partly tied to the Ley Juárez. Hamnett reads the closed cathedral doors as a trap meant to make the new government begin with coercive action. Juárez did not force the doors; after unsuccessful correspondence with the bishop he imposed only nominal fines, ignored the closure, and turned the inauguration into a civil ceremony without religious rites thereafter.

p.101 then frames Juárez's public-private line: public officials need not attend religious ceremonies in their official capacity, while private religious practice remained possible. Hamnett interprets this as a republican principle: civil power should not adopt any one religion, should defend liberty, and should govern for the electorate rather than the Church.

Juárez further angered the bishop by using Bernardino Carvajal, a cleric who had left active pastoral work and moved into civil service. Hamnett presents Carvajal as a liberal Oaxaca political figure, with state-senate, Ayutla, legal-education, and state-constitution connections.

The page then shifts to ministerial enforcement against clerical political action. Pablo de la Llave ordered governors on 1857-04-01 to send clergy who abused the pulpit for political purposes and endangered public order to Mexico City. In late June 1857 Juárez ordered the arrest and removal of the parish priest of the sierra mining town Zoochila. The priest had refused last rites and Christian burial to a Tavehua village justice because the man had not retracted his constitutional oath.

p.101 closes by beginning a new section on escalation toward the Reform War. The sentence about Federal Congress granting Comonfort extraordinary powers and suppressing clerical disobedience breaks after "denial of"; this was resolved on p.102 in [[CAP_HAMNETT_JUAREZ_0054]] and recorded as [[FACT_HAMNETT_JUAREZ_1180]].

## Shawcross Cross-Check

- Maximilian-centered narration can compress Reform Laws into anti-Church policy; this page frames Juárez's position as public authority separated from religious ceremony, not religious denial.
- Domínguez did not begin in total confrontation: he performed two Te Deums despite legal and canonical objections.
- Hamnett reads the cathedral-clergy refusal as political pressure designed to damage the new government at its start.
- Sacrament and burial refusal links political loyalty, salvation, public order, and state sovereignty.
- Comonfort extraordinary powers begin at the page end and should be checked against Shawcross's imperial prehistory only after the sentence is complete.
- Juárez government action should be read through public authority, public order, and republican principle rather than as a simple anti-religious move.
