## Summary

p.230 resolves the prior-page unfinished "At" by placing Félix Díaz's killing at Chacalapa, followed by body mutilation. This completes the capture-to-killing sequence without adding details beyond this page and without treating the violence as spectacle.

After San Mateo Xindihui, Porfirio Díaz fled in disguise to Veracruz, received help from Teodoro Dehesa for passage to New York on 1 February 1872, crossed the United States by rail, and moved through San Francisco, Manzanillo, and Tepic while seeking support. Hamnett then presents Manuel Lozada as refusing to side with either Liberal faction, even while rejecting Juárez government legitimacy on grounds different from Díaz's anti-reelection argument and warning that Liberal civil conflict could invite United States intervention or incorporation.

The spread turns back to Oaxaca. Juárez used the collapse of the Díaz brothers' position to restore juarista control, wanted Miguel Castro returned to the governorship, urged juchitecos to cooperate with Castro, and supported fresh state congressional elections to exclude Díaz supporters. Alatorre removed Romero from the acting governorship on 8 January 1872, assumed political and military control, nominated Castro as provisional governor, and federal appointment followed on 16 January. A new Oaxaca Congress met in June, and Juárez congratulated Castro.

Juárez's Oaxaca reorganization is also framed as federal-state intervention, not only legality restoration. He feared renewed Tehuantepec-Juchitán conflict, separation pressure, and risk around the old Federal Territory of the Isthmus of Tehuantepec because he considered the inter-oceanic route strategically important. The juchitecos' request that a new St Vincent image be funded from Díaz's estate is kept as a religious-symbolic and local-memory issue.

p.231 follows the northern military endgame. Treviño threatened San Luis Potosí and the Bajío in January 1872; Rocha took Aguascalientes on 29 February, defeated Zacatecas rebels three days later, and entered Monterrey on 9 July, ending Treviño's rebellion. Porfirio Díaz modified the Plan of La Noria at Ameca on 3 April to attract lerdista support by opening immediate succession to Lerdo, but Hamnett says the move failed to broaden support or split loyal generals.

Hamnett closes with an interpretation: La Noria was badly conceived politically and militarily, Juárez outmanoeuvred Díaz, Díaz's armed rebellion drove lerdistas back toward Juárez, many non-juaristas refused a revolt that threatened the Constitution of 1857, and Juárez could rely on loyal governors and commanders. In Oaxaca, Hamnett attributes Félix Díaz's ruin to Castro, sierra militia, and juchiteco intervention.
