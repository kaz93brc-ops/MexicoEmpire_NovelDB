## Summary

Hamnett uses pp.180-181 to shift from the vulnerability of the juarista position to the northern supports that helped Juárez survive. Sonora, Sinaloa, Durango, and Chihuahua are treated as a rear base, and Hamnett interprets juarismo as acquiring a northern character through the 1865-66 crisis.

The spread then turns to Juárez's northern political network. Terrazas remains important beyond 1867; Escobedo is framed as an anti-Vidaurri ally; Juárez appoints Escobedo to military and state authority; and Jesús Carranza appears as a Coahuila supporter recommended to Escobedo.

Hamnett also deepens the theme of Juárez's distrust of his own camp. Juárez is shown as skeptical of Liberal politicians and generals who might compromise with the French or Empire to preserve their own positions, a distrust Hamnett uses to explain why Juárez clung to power without reducing it to simple ambition.

The p.181 military material records guerrilla and counter-guerrilla warfare in Veracruz, Huasteca, and Guerrero. It closes with Guerrero politics: the Chilapa relief, Santacilia report, Diego Álvarez's continuation in office, the state of siege of 27 August 1866, and the later rupture with the Jiménez clientele.

- Juárez privately weighing whether compromise-minded Liberals might remove him.

- Guerrero victory producing relief while Juárez still senses the Álvarez-Jiménez alliance is unstable.
- Counter-guerrilla uniforms symbolizing the collapse of clear boundaries between regular and irregular war.
