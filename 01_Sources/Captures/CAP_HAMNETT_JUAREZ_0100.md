## Summary

Juárez and Sebastián Lerdo de Tejada, travelling incognito, visited the church where Maximilian's body was laid out. Hamnett says this was the first time Juárez directly saw Maximilian. Juárez made only a short observation on the body's appearance to Lerdo and did not expand on it. The following day, the body was robed or arranged, and photography was permitted.

The chapter conclusion then follows post-execution reactions. Appeals for Maximilian's life from European figures such as Giuseppe Garibaldi and Victor Hugo reached Mexico after the execution, even though both were sympathetic to Juárez and the Mexican republican cause. Some Liberal politicians praised the government's insistence on execution. Juan José Baz argued that Maximilian had to be sacrificed to remove the principle he represented.

Juárez issued a Manifesto defending his conduct. The Manifesto framed Maximilian as a criminal against the Anáhuac nation, contrasted European absolute monarchies with American republics, invoked Anáhuac and Aztec historical memory, condemned European colonial morality, and denied Maximilian legitimate sovereign status. Hamnett interprets this language as evidence that Zapotec Juárez had absorbed a Mexico City-centered nationalist construct.

The page also records the postwar refusal of diplomatic relations with European powers that had supported the Empire. The British mission closed while consuls remained outside the capital; relations with Great Britain resumed only in 1884. Spain and France returned to relations in 1871 after regime changes in Europe. Hamnett closes with a critical evaluation of the capture, trial, execution, legal procedure, political purpose, sacrifice motif, and telegraphic speed of orders.
