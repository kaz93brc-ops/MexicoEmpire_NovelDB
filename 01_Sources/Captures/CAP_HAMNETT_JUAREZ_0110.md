## Summary

pp.214-215 close the Guerrero civil-war section and open Hamnett's discussion of social movements and the agrarian question. Jiménez's La Noria role is completed as organization of porfirista forces across Guerrero, coastal Oaxaca, and the Putla direction, with an attempted junction with Félix Díaz.

Hamnett then shifts to village land rights after the Empire. He argues that many Indigenous peasant communities lost expectations that government would recognize communal ownership or corporate rights after the Liberal return. Maximilian's 1866-06-26 decree and the Junta for the Protection of the Impoverished Classes are treated as limited but significant imperial-era protection mechanisms, not a wholesale reversal of Liberal privatization.

The restored Liberal government dismantled imperial peasant-protection procedures, while central Mexico saw resistance to the reintroduced Liberal order. Hamnett links the 1867-12-18 suspension of constitutional guarantees, Chalco-area hacienda pressure, railway-related land value increases, and Julio Chávez López's 1868 rebellion. He separates Chávez López's career and execution from the interpretation of his expectations, disappointment in the Juárez government, and possible anarchist influence.

By p.215, federal troops have suppressed the main rebellion and its spread through Morelos, Puebla, Tlaxcala, Mezquital, and Hidalgo. Hamnett interprets 1868-1870 resistance as delaying rather than reversing Liberal policy and as frustrating policy supporters. He then connects broader economic stress and land sales to Mariano Riva Palacio's 1870 Chalco sale before moving to Juárez's 1868-05-08 additional extraordinary powers and revival of the 1862 punitive law for the García de la Cadena rebellion.
