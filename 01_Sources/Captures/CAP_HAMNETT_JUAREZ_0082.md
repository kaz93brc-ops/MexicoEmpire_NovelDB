## Summary

p.158 completes Lerdo's warning that Romero should not obtain U.S. assistance through any arrangement harming Mexican territorial integrity. Hamnett then presents Juárez as welcoming Lincoln's refusal to compromise with the Confederacy, the destruction of slavery, non-recognition of Maximilian's empire, and the blow to Napoleon III's policy.

Juárez's 6 April 1865 letter to Santacilia is used as evidence that he expected Mexico to continue fighting France alone and wear the French down. The new subsection argues that the Monroe Doctrine had little practical effect before Union victory, but Juárez welcomed Andrew Johnson because Johnson was viewed as a Monroe Doctrine supporter.

Hamnett links Confederate survival to a possible coexistence with the Mexican Empire, but notes that French setbacks in Coahuila and Nuevo León already encouraged Juárez before Richmond and Lee's surrender. Juárez then remained at El Paso del Norte from 14 August to 13 November 1865 and declined residence on the New Mexico side.

pp.158-159 treats Maximilian's policy toward defeated Confederates and the 5 September 1865 Colonization Law. The law's free-status language and its restrictive labor-contract provisions are separated, and Romero's critique of the policy as disguised southern slavery is preserved as a primary-testimony candidate.

p.159 then follows the United States appointment of a minister to Republican Mexico, French proposals to trade withdrawal for U.S. recognition of the Empire, Seward's refusal to recognize a regime opposed to Juárez, pressure from Union and radical actors, Johnson's demobilization, Napoleon III's January 1866 phased withdrawal order, continued Seward obstruction of arms purchases, arms-price pressure in summer 1866, and U.S. congressional non-recognition of González Ortega in late August 1866.
