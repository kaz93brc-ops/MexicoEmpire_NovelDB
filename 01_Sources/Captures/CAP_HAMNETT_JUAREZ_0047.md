## Summary

p.88 opens by resolving the prior page's unfinished Oaxaca church-institution argument. Hamnett explains the relative retreat of the Dominicans / Order of Preachers partly through episcopal policy that transferred parishes to secular clergy. This completes the immediate p.87 continuation, while the broader Dominican decline remains tied to the combined pp.87-88 context.

Hamnett then widens the frame to early nineteenth-century Mexico. He identifies the strongest Catholic region as a central-northwestern belt including Querétaro, Michoacán, Jalisco, Zacatecas, San Luis Potosí, and Guanajuato. He describes a rooted Catholic popular culture that was neither simply Spanish nor indigenous, and places the Guadalajara-Querétaro core among the most urbanized, populous, intellectually developed, and economically active regions. The independence movement arising there lets Hamnett argue that Catholicism and early nationalism were not inherently opposed.

Hamnett treats 1750-1850 as a century of relative decline and crisis for the Mexican Catholic Church, connected to Enlightenment thought, rupture with Spain, and independent state formation. He separates this longer crisis from the more direct Liberal onslaught of 1855-1875. The crisis is presented as intellectual, spiritual, and political: fewer clerical vocations, decline of male religious orders, Enlightenment criticism of scholasticism, collapse of Spanish rule, clergy divisions over independence, state attempts to control bishop appointment, and Liberal extension of Bourbon church subordination.

The page gives numerical indicators of early post-independence weakening: a decline in clerical candidates and Church property between 1808 and 1825, a priest-number decline from 1810 to 1830, and the departure or death of many bishops including the archbishop of Mexico. By the 1840s, however, the Church had begun to recover, and a reconstructed episcopate could oppose Reform-period Liberal policy.

p.89 shifts to Liberal and Church perspectives. The Reform generation inherited Mora, Zavala, and Gómez Farías but faced sharper issues of Church and State, debt, and disamortization. In the 1850s, Liberals split over whether the Church should be subordinated to the state in Bourbon fashion or separated from it in a secularized society. Hamnett links Mexican Liberalism's focus on the Church question to European free-Church ideas, while also explaining why Catholic hierarchy, clergy, and many believers saw Liberal policy as a threat to Church revival, Mexican identity, and Catholic unity.

Hamnett also situates Pius IX's view in an Italian and Catholic-world frame: nationalism, national churches, and state-centered political religion made Mexican Liberalism look like part of a broader threat. He says Pius IX saw Comonfort, Ocampo, Prieto, Lerdo, and Juárez through an analogy with Mazzini and Garibaldi. From the Liberal side, Hamnett says Church corporate privilege was read as an old-regime obstacle to intellectual emancipation and social modernization. With reference to Hobsbawm, he frames Mexican Liberalism as building an alternative value-world grounded in republican values, national identity, civic virtue, and lay public education. The p.89 Chadwick sentence is unfinished and must be held for screenshot 48.
