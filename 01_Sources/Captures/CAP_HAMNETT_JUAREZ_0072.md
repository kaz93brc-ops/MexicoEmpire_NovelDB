## Summary

pp.138-139 close the Chihuahua dispute by showing Juárez's tactical reconciliation with Luis Terrazas. Because Chihuahua remained under siege, Terrazas could not take office after the June 1865 election; Juárez restored him to the governorship and military command on 7 November 1865, which Hamnett interprets as the only workable way to preserve Juárez's political footing inside Chihuahua.

The spread then opens the November 1865 succession crisis. Juárez's constitutional presidential term was due to expire, elections could not be held during the civil war, and González Ortega's Supreme Court office made him the rival succession claimant. Hamnett presents Juárez and Lerdo as building a legal-political case against Ortega through non-exercise of office, the Supreme Court's inactivity after May 1863, and article 118's incompatibility rule.

On p.139, Hamnett follows Ortega's U.S. stay, Romero and Santacilia's surveillance, Lerdo's 28 October 1865 instruction to governors, and the two 8 November decrees extending both the presidential term and the Supreme Court presidency for the war's duration. Ortega's protest and Hamnett's analysis are kept separate: Ortega called the action unconstitutional and usurpatory, while Hamnett stresses Juárez's backing among northern governors and military commanders and begins the argument that this was not a presidential coup.

The page also traces costs and aftermath: Juárez's realpolitik alienated Ruiz and Prieto, fixed Ortega and Miguel Negrete as enemies, drew Romero and Seward into blocking Ortega's return, and led to Ortega and Patoni's arrest in Zacatecas in January 1867 and custody until 18 July 1868.

## Shawcross Cross-Check

- This spread counters a Maximilian-centered account by showing that republican survival also depended on internal succession politics, emergency powers, state-governor support, and military command alignment.
- Juárez's term extension should not be reduced to personal power hunger; the civil war, French intervention, election impossibility, Ortega succession claim, and fear of imperial compromise remain part of the context.
- Ortega's protest should also remain visible, so Juárez's legality is not treated as automatically settled.
- The passage is a Liberal-internal conflict over law, succession, military force, and diplomacy rather than a simple Liberal/Conservative divide.
- Patoni's shift from Juárez's anti-Terrazas instrument to Ortega-side arrestee is a useful warning against fixed faction labels.
