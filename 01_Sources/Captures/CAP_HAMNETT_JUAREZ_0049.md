## Summary

p.92 completes the previous page's explanation of papal non-recognition by connecting the broken phrase to Spanish crown. Hamnett then uses Oaxaca diocesan figures to show clergy, priest, and parish decline before Mantecón's arrival.

Dr Antonio Mantecón's July 1844 arrival is presented as the real restoration of the Oaxaca episcopal office. Hamnett stresses his local elite roots, legal and municipal background, and diocesan administration until February 1852. Juárez is then framed as the first governor to face a settled bishop in Oaxaca.

Hamnett argues that Juárez did not act from ideological hostility to religion itself. His aims are described as civil power over clergy and removal of clerical predominance in education, but he sought a practical modus vivendi with Mantecón rather than direct confrontation. The proposed line was civil law over clergy while leaving internal discipline to ecclesiastical courts; Hamnett does not treat this as a rupture with the Holy See, apostolic succession, or doctrine.

The sources do not reveal how Mantecón viewed Juárez's Zapotec origin, masonic affiliation, or Liberal politics. Hamnett instead stresses Juárez's insecure political environment: distrust of the military, uncertainty about clergy, Isthmus instability, and continuing U.S. military threat.

Juárez's primary education policy extended beyond district towns where possible into Indian areas. Hamnett treats Oaxacan Liberal education as part of a state-led laicization project that sought to detach Indigenous majorities from what Liberal elites called inherited superstition, while also noting chronic state weakness and scarce public resources.

p.93 turns to private donations at Ocotlán and Tehuantepec, the Santa Ana Zegache school fund, Juárez's July 1848 report of 476 functioning schools, and the shared interest of Juárez and Mantecón in disciplining secular clergy and Dominican friars. It then examines parish dues: villages were obliged to support parish clergy, but their resistance was not simply irreligion because village income also supported confraternities. The page ends as Juárez's 24 January 1849 instruction to department governors begins; the quoted instruction is not complete until the next screenshot.

## Shawcross Cross-Check

- Maximilian-centered narration can make Reforma appear as an attack on the Church, but Hamnett frames Juárez in Oaxaca as pursuing civil power, education policy, law, and practical coexistence rather than immediate total confrontation.
- Juárez's church policy is not denial of religion itself; it places clergy under civil law while leaving internal discipline partly to church courts.
- The Catholic Church is not one actor with one will; Mantecón could share some practical interests with the state government.
- Village resistance to clergy dues can coexist with local religious spending through confraternities.
- Oaxacan Liberal education policy contains a paternal, top-down civilizing and laicizing impulse toward Indigenous majorities.
- The page helps explain why imperial or conservative voices could read Liberal reforms as religious destruction, while the local mechanisms were more tangled.
