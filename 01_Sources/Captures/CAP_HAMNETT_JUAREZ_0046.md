## Summary

Chapter 5 opens by framing Juárez and the Catholic Church through Liberalism, masonic networks, secularization, education, corporate privilege, and church-state relations rather than simple irreligion. Hamnett says Juárez, like other Liberal leaders, was a freemason and places masonic affiliations within Mexican party divisions since the 1820s. He also notes François-Xavier Guerra's attention to masonic lodges as connective nodes in the Reforma and Restored Republic periods, and says Maximilian was also a mason.

Hamnett does not present Juárez as simply atheist, deist, or anti-Catholic. He stresses that Juárez retained some Catholic social practice, including church marriage, while seeking to remove the political role and educational control of the Catholic hierarchy and to secularize social and political institutions.

p.87 turns to church-state relations as a major source of Mexican political division. Hamnett treats the dispute as institutional and constitutional, involving ecclesiastical and secular power, religious toleration, Catholic exclusivity, fueros, immigration, modernization, and fear of turning Mexico into a replica of the United States.

Hamnett then describes the colonial Catholic Church as a powerful institution in Mexico, centered especially on Mexico City and Puebla, while noting weak rural penetration and hybrid Catholic practice in indigenous communities. For Oaxaca, he highlights regional difference: even in 1910 Spanish speakers were only 51 percent of the Diocese of Oaxaca population, and hybrid Catholicism coexisted with village religious institutions. Oaxaca City had significant ecclesiastical institutions, but not on the scale of Puebla or Mexico City. The explanation of Dominican / Order of Preachers decline in Oaxaca continues onto the next page.
