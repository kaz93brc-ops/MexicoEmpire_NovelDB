## Summary

p.210 continues the Puebla section by presenting Cravioto's early-1869 advice to Juárez: Zacapoaxtla and Zacatlán had to be secured to keep Puebla and Veracruz from deteriorating. Hamnett connects this military concern to García's enforcement of the 1863 land law in the Huauchinango district, which he interprets as aggravating village and land disputes and pushing the district toward rebellion.

Negrete then appears in Puebla city on 3 February 1869 under protection from Méndez supporters and calls for rebellion against the Juárez administration. His charges target the 1865 coup d'état, presidential dictatorship, the convocatoria, and interference with authorities, legislatures, and elections. His plan demands strict observance of the 1857 Constitution, new elections, and replacement of authorities. Hamnett treats this as an anticipation of the later Plan of La Noria and as evidence of cooperation between Negrete and Puebla sierra chiefs.

Negrete failed to recruit Félix Díaz, who shared some anti-Juárez language but, in Hamnett's reading, avoided a movement likely to fail. Alatorre defeated Negrete at Tepeji, but Negrete escaped with about 200 followers. The unrest continued: Lucas rose at Xochiapulco, occupied Zacapoaxtla, and the cost of operations led Alatorre and Cravioto to insist that the federal government, not the Puebla state treasury, would have to pay.

Hamnett's broader interpretation is that Puebla rebels could damage Juárez by diverting reconstruction funds to security, giving the state Congress an opposition base, and weakening García and Romero Vargas, who lacked enough local support to counter Reform-era sierra chiefs. Puebla remained unstable through 1870, entered the La Noria movement, and still troubled the federal government in spring 1872. On 5 March 1872 Juárez placed Puebla under siege and removed Romero Vargas's civil authority; after La Noria collapsed, Alatorre became Military Commander and Governor.

p.211 then begins "THE CIVIL WAR IN GUERRERO." Hamnett frames Juárez as drawn into a power struggle between the Álvarez clientele and Vicente Jiménez supporters. The Jiménez supporters are identified as porfiristas and linked to opposition in the federal Congress, but the sentence ends unfinished and is not independently carded here.
