## Summary

Hamnett distinguishes Juárez's 8 November 1865 decrees from Comonfort's armed constitutional overthrow. He reads the decrees as an emergency effort to preserve executive continuity after other republican institutions had lapsed, while keeping Ortega-side constitutional criticism separate.

Juárez had tried to show that the loss of Mexico City did not destroy republican institutions by convening Congress in San Luis Potosí. The Supreme Court had convened there in June 1863, but the 1865 congressional summons failed because attendance never reached quorum. The Permanent Deputation's 27 November 1865 manifesto is presented as an explanation of the summons rather than as restored legislative power.

Hamnett then links northern politics to the anti-Imperial recovery: the Terrazas-Juárez relationship helped Juárez maintain northern authority, oppose Vidaurri, and push back French power. Escobedo reorganized the Army of the North, recovered Monterrey, and, with Díaz's recovery of Oaxaca, became part of the military architecture of Liberal victory. Vidaurri's 1867 execution by Díaz's forces is preserved as a separate hard-edged Republican-side fact.

p.141 stresses that Juárez worked both with and against governors and used short tactical alliances to curb independent politicians. Hamnett says the 1857 Constitution formally remained in force and elections occurred where possible, but Juárez's extraordinary powers placed him in practice both within and above the Constitution. The passage closes by treating the decrees' central significance as Juárez's determination to fight Maximilian's Empire to the end, then turns to family sacrifice and the unfinished topic of Juárez's ruthlessness.

## Shawcross Cross-Check

- This spread corrects a Maximilian-centered collapse narrative by showing Juárez-side institutional improvisation, executive emergency power, state-governor management, and northern military recovery.
- The 8 November decrees can look like Juárez's power retention from the Imperial side; Hamnett instead stresses the determination to keep fighting Maximilian's Empire, while the constitutional criticism remains visible.
- Escobedo's Monterrey recovery, Díaz's Oaxaca recovery, and Vidaurri's execution should be connected to later Querétaro, Maximilian's capture, and Republican victory without turning Liberal violence into a moral blank check.
- Juárez family sacrifice can be contrasted with Maximilian/Carlota tragedy only as a narrative parallel, not as a replacement for source-based evidence.
