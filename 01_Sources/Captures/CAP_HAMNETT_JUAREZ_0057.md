## Summary

p.108 continues Ruiz's circular defense of the Veracruz Reform Laws. Ruiz framed confiscation of Church property as a way to distinguish religion from clerical abuse and corporate privilege, not as destruction of religion, and denied that the Church had to be treated as a complete association requiring state protection.

The same spread records the reform package around civil marriage, Civil Registry, burial and funeral jurisdiction, official celebrations, religious ceremonies, clerical dress, church bells, taxation of clergy, and liberty of religious belief. Hamnett separates Juárez's and Ruiz's arguments from later simple anti-religious readings, while stressing that many Catholics were shocked and that Conservative authorities could use the measures as propaganda.

p.109 also says Juárez welcomed civil marriage, registered his newborn son in the Veracruz Registry, and used a letter to Doblado to reject Conservative accusations against Liberals. Hamnett treats Juárez's positive reference to French revolutionary radicals as idealizing 1790s France while also exposing the distance between Veracruz in 1859 and Paris in 1793.

The Bishop of Oaxaca death story is kept as rumor or political report, not as confirmed historical fact. p.109 ends with "Yet, on the other hand, he ..."; this continuation is resolved in [[CAP_HAMNETT_JUAREZ_0058]] as Hamnett's point that Juárez still saw the Church as part of domestic political balance.
