## Summary

p.222 resolves the previous-page fragment: during suppression of the La Noria rebellion, Juárez used loyal federal commanders to neutralize opposition military groups threatening his government. Hamnett interprets Juárez as having benefited from enemy divisions and as believing by spring 1872 that serious threats to his position had been destroyed.

The new Oaxaca section treats Juárez's home state as a major post-Imperial danger. Hamnett says Juárez lost effective control of Oaxaca from November 1867 until Félix Díaz's fall in November 1871. Porfirio Díaz's prestige from the December 1866 recovery of Oaxaca City and Félix Díaz's political skill helped move the 10 November 1867 governorship election away from the juaristas and toward Félix Díaz, known as El Chato, against Miguel Castro.

Hamnett presents the election as a village, municipal, and district struggle over committees, voter eligibility, counting, and the mobilizing work of `jefes políticos`. Félix Díaz won a large reported vote margin, though Castro retained strength in the sierra. Oaxaca differed from Puebla because the Díaz brothers could not rely personally on the sierra militias, which Hamnett places under Francisco Meijueiro and Fidencio Hernández and mainly loyal to Castro and Juárez. Ixtlán mining interests and War Minister Ignacio Mejía also opposed or distrusted the Díaz brothers.

p.223 follows Félix Díaz's state machinery: he replaced the Castro-aligned editor of `La Victoria` in 1868, turned the paper into a vehicle for anti-clerical attacks, and reached a statewide church-bell prohibition. Hamnett treats this anti-clerical and authoritarian posture as politically damaging and ultimately dangerous to El Chato.

Juárez still had Oaxaca informants. Joaquín Mauleón, a relative and state deputy whom Hamnett treats with reliability caveats, reported juarista survival and the October 1869 reconciliation of the Díaz brothers at Félix's son's baptism. Roberto Maqueo appears as a businessman tied to both sides. Félix Romero became more important: Juárez placed him for state Supreme Court succession strategy, and Romero entered Díaz's governing center as secretary from 7 January 1870. Hamnett closes the spread by describing Díaz's appointment machine, 6 November 1869 electoral success, weak federal authority in Oaxaca, National Guard reconstruction after 1867, and El Chato's roughly 3,000 armed men funded by capitation-tax income. The final sentence continues onto the next page.

## Shawcross Cross-Check

### Maximilian Centered Narrative Correction

- Republican victory after Maximilian did not produce a settled order; Oaxaca shows post-Imperial Liberal state competition.
- Porfirio Díaz's anti-Imperial military prestige became political capital inside the restored Republic.

### Juárez / Republican View

- Juárez fought La Noria while also using informants, court placement, and loyal federal commanders against Díaz-controlled Oaxaca.
- The conflict is Republican and Liberal internal politics, not simply Empire versus Republic.

### Liberal / Conservative Binary

- Félix Díaz, Miguel Castro, Juárez, Romero, and their followers operate inside broad Liberal politics.
- The operative issues are electoral control, militia command, administrative appointments, anti-clerical policy, and federal authority.

### Catholic Church / Army / Regional Power / Popular Politics

- Catholic Church: `La Victoria`, attacks on clergy, and church-bell prohibition.
- Army: federal commanders, Oaxaca National Guard reconstruction, and El Chato's armed force.
- Regional power: Oaxaca state government controlled electoral machinery while federal authority remained weak.
- Popular politics: village elections, `jefes políticos`, and likely local reaction to the church-bell prohibition.

## Creative Use

### historical_use

- Juárez realizes that one of the gravest threats after La Noria comes from his home state.
- Félix Díaz uses Porfirio's prestige while building his own local machine.
- Village committees, district vote counts, and `jefes políticos` make elections feel like local combat rather than a simple ballot count.
- `La Victoria` and the church-bell prohibition show anti-clerical reform becoming coercive state practice.
- Juárez reads Mauleón and Romero reports to monitor Oaxaca from a distance.
- Roberto Maqueo can function as a practical intermediary connected to both camps.

### creative_inference

- Juárez silently absorbs reports that his own Oaxaca is outside his control.
- Félix Díaz frames church-bell prohibition as reform while villages read it as intrusion.
- Romero enters Díaz's office and sees personnel, correspondence, and appointments from the inside.
- Porfirio and Félix can be written as brothers whose cooperation never removes rivalry.
- Maqueo can be shown as a businessman testing which side will prevail.
