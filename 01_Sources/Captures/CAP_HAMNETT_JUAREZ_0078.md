## Summary

p.150 completes the Republican-party continuation by noting that Lincoln and other later Republican leaders had opposed the 1846 war and southern expansionism. Hamnett then follows Buchanan's search for a policy success: Churchwell inferred possible concessions from Lerdo and Ocampo, and Buchanan sent Robert McLane to Veracruz with authority over recognition, frontier questions, Lower California payment, and transit rights. McLane recognized the Juárez regime on 1859-04-06.

Mata's Washington reception and warning show the double edge of recognition: he wanted U.S.-Mexican futures tied together but also warned that territorial appetite in Washington had become dangerous. Hamnett presents Juárez as expecting recognition to strengthen Liberal moral standing and loan prospects, while McLane resisted church-property loan security because it would reduce U.S. leverage over territory and transit. Juárez, in Hamnett's reading, avoided Lower California cession partly because a Santa Anna/Mesilla-style stigma would damage his government.

p.150-p.151 widens the issue beyond the U.S. border. Spanish, British, and French naval pressure over debt made European involvement a major cause of U.S. anxiety. Hamnett frames the McLane-Ocampo Treaty as the result of Washington's fears and Juárez's immediate military need under Miramón's Veracruz threat. The treaty was signed on 1859-12-14, conceived as an offensive and defensive alliance, but was not ratified by either side; Juárez formally rejected it in November 1860 after the Conservative danger had largely passed.
