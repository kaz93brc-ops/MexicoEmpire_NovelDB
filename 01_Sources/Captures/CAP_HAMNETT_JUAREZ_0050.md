## Summary

p.94 completes the 24 January 1849 Juárez administration instruction begun on p.93. The completed argument links Liberal and republican institutions to duties, obedience, respect for law, strict observance of law, and restraint on abuses of liberty; this continuity was added to [[FACT_HAMNETT_JUAREZ_1074]] and [[TIME_HAMNETT_JUAREZ_0399]] rather than duplicated.

Hamnett then shows Juárez and Mantecón cooperating over roads to Tehuantepec and Huatulco, while interpreting that cooperation as evidence that both the state government and the Church had limited ability to implement policy locally without each other.

The Zimatlán parish-dues dispute presents Juárez's legal-order liberalism in a village context: Mantecón complained about dues nonpayment, Juárez ordered a Prefect report, and Hamnett reads Juárez as morally sympathetic to peasant distress but unwilling to accept political disobedience.

pp.94-95 then shift to latent Church-state suspicion. A late-summer 1849 cholera threat and poor hospital conditions led Juárez to press Mantecón over hospital repairs and the Hospice of the Poor; Manuel Ruiz's failed approaches and written exchange with Mantecón made civil authority itself a point of conflict.

p.95 closes with Church wealth and statistical inquiry. Juárez sought information on churches and contents through parish priests and department governors, while Mantecón answered that rural clergy were unprepared, many parishes were poor, and Indigenous communities could read the inquiry as hostile. The final sentence about pressure on the Church's corporate position and village hostility continues to the next screenshot and is not Fact-carded here.

## Shawcross Cross-Check

- Maximilian-centered narration can make Reforma and Church policy look mainly like an attack on the Church, but Hamnett here frames Juárez through Oaxaca administration, law, public works, hospitals, Church property statistics, and village burdens.
- Juárez's Liberalism is administrative and legalistic: duty, law observance, obedience, and republican citizenship matter as much as abstract liberty.
- Juárez and Mantecón cooperate and clash; Church-state relations are not a simple total confrontation.
- Church-property inquiry touches village religious contributions and the Church's corporate position, not only elite Church wealth.
- Village people may resist state intervention into Church property or religious contributions without becoming anti-religious.
- The 1849-50 material gives a pre-Reforma line for later 1855-57 conflict without making it a direct cause.
