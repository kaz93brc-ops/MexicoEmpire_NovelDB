## Summary

p.192 continues the defence argument that Juárez's government had fought for constitutional principles yet was now treating the 1862 law and emergency powers as overriding normal protections. After failing to dissuade the government, the defence moved to clemency on 1867-06-12. Two days later a specially appointed court martial sentenced Maximilian to death for crimes against the nation, popular rights, order, and public peace. Hamnett notes that many charges were political, while some, including the 1865 decree, followed from Maximilian's political situation.

The page then follows diplomatic and legal pleas. Baron Anton von Magnus appealed for pardon, but Lerdo rejected clemency by arguing that a living Maximilian might return with European backing and that republican institutions had to be respected abroad. On 1867-06-15 Mariano Riva Palacio and Rafael Martínez de la Torre told Juárez that a death sentence did not require execution and warned that killing Maximilian would isolate Mexico morally and diplomatically. Juárez answered that Escobedo had to confirm the sentence before the government considered pardon.

On 1867-06-16 the defence appealed again to Juárez as Liberal leader and family man. Lerdo read the telegram denying pardon, and execution was postponed until the morning of 1867-06-19 so the prisoners could settle their affairs. Ortega and Vázquez reported from Querétaro that the prisoners had made their last confessions and were prepared to die; Hamnett says the delay made them feel as if they would `die twice`.

p.193 records final pleas and aftermath. On 1867-06-18 Maximilian telegraphed Juárez asking for Miramón and Mejía rather than himself. Miramón's wife reached San Luis Potosí with weeping women, but Juárez refused to receive her and said an interview would only worsen her suffering because the court martial decision was irrevocable. Martínez warned against further bloodshed; Juárez thanked the counsel but said law and sentence were then `inexorable`.

Maximilian, believing Carlota dead, thanked counsel and wrote that his life could be laid down if it ended civil war, asking that his sacrifice be the last bloodshed and bring peace. At 7:05 a.m. on 1867-06-19 Maximilian, Miramón, and Mejía were executed at Cerro de las Campanas. Hamnett then follows the body's delayed burial, the Mexican government's guarded embalming and anti-veneration concern, Austrian and Prussian requests, Vice-Admiral Tegetthoff's 1867-09-01 arrival, and the unresolved body-transfer sentence continuing to the next page.
