## Summary

p.160 completes the imperialist propaganda argument by presenting European alliance as a counterweight to United States expansion. Imperialist press rhetoric compared U.S. expansion with Russian imperial threat, justified French intervention through a Crimea-style analogy, warned that U.S. victory threatened Canada and Cuba, and used McLane-Ocampo, 1847, Mesilla, treatment of former Mexican residents, Juárez's 1865 extension, and Manuel Ruiz's desertion against the Liberal government.

The spread then shifts to Juárez's Liberal self-understanding. Hamnett frames Juárez as seeing Mexican history since 1821 as a struggle between privilege and liberty, while Juárez's 16 December 1866 New York Herald interview connects Liberal politics to recovering government from ecclesiastical and military privilege and to the 1824 and 1857 constitutional line.

p.161 adds the March 1868 letter to a German liberal, then qualifies U.S. aid. Hamnett argues that Juárez linked his cause to U.S. founding principles but received mostly moral support; Zarco's September 1866 letter warned that even that support had been late and unstable and that Mexican resistance, not the United States, deserved the principal credit.

The page closes with later evaluations by Fernando Iglesias Calderón and Hilarión Frías y Soto. Frías attacked U.S. neutrality claims, tied U.S. caution to fear of French recognition of the Confederacy, and defended Juárez against territorial-concession accusations by attributing the 1864 idea to Romero and Doblado and by noting that Baja California and Sonora were not effectively available to Juárez's government.
