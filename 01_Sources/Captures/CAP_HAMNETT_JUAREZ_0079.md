## Summary

p.152 completes Hamnett's McLane-Ocampo Treaty argument: the treaty did not cede Lower/Baja California, Sonora, or Chihuahua, but transit concessions were still a serious survival price paid to preserve the Liberal regime, republican government, the 1857 Constitution, and the Reform Laws under fear of European intervention and monarchy. Hamnett distinguishes legal sovereignty from political reputation damage: Ocampo's reputation suffered, Juárez inherited a durable betrayal charge, and conservative and clerical opponents used the treaty to portray him as anti-religious and pro-U.S. Protestant enemy.

The short-term military context was acute. Miramón's November 1859 successes showed, in Hamnett's reading, that the Liberals could not win alone; in April 1860 Juárez secured U.S. naval intervention against Spanish ships contracted by Miramón's regime, the episode known as the Antón Lizardo Incident. Long-term, U.S. political change and Senate sectional conflict made the treaty unlikely to take effect.

p.152-p.153 begins the Civil War section. Hamnett presents Matías Romero as Juárez's close Washington minister from 1860 to 1868 and treats his selection as evidence of Juárez's personnel judgment. Romero reported on U.S. politics, expected Republican victory after the Democratic split, feared U.S. expansion and Cuba annexation, saw secession as a republican calamity, but also calculated that Union-Confederate rivalry might reduce the danger of total U.S. absorption of Mexico. He expected Republican sympathy and Lincoln's administration to protect Mexican rights, while viewing Seward as crucial but susceptible to European diplomatic influence.

## Shawcross Cross-Check

- Hamnett adds a pre-Empire U.S. diplomacy layer before Shawcross's stronger emphasis on later U.S. pressure against Maximilian and France.
- Juárez and Romero should not be written as simple pro-U.S. actors: they feared U.S. expansion while exploiting U.S. party politics and anti-European interests.
- Seward's caution toward European Powers helps correct any simple picture of immediate U.S. anti-French intervention.
- Conservative and clerical anti-Juárez propaganda connects treaty politics with later Imperial and Church-side anti-republican rhetoric.

## Creative Use

### Historical Use

- Veracruz siege diplomacy can show Juárez relying on U.S. naval help while refusing territorial cession.
- Ocampo can carry the political cost of a treaty that did not legally cede territory but damaged reputation.

- Romero can be used as a Washington operator reading party politics, Union prospects, Confederate leverage, European threat, and Seward's temperament.
- The Civil War can create the irony that U.S. expansion danger recedes just as European intervention danger grows.
