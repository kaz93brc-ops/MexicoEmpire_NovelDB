## Summary

pp.220-221 open Chapter 10 by framing presidential re-election as a recurring Latin American problem tied to self-perpetuating power and personal rule. Hamnett then places Juárez's 1867 and 1871 re-elections in a longer Mexican context: the 1824 and 1857 constitutions did not explicitly bar re-election, while later Porfirian and revolutionary politics made anti-re-election a central slogan.

Hamnett treats Juárez's re-election as exceptional to contemporaries because Mexico had rarely seen full presidential terms or peaceful transfers before 1867. He uses Guadalupe Victoria, José Joaquín de Herrera, and Mariano Arista to illustrate that rarity, then argues that post-1821 Mexico lacked a settled mechanism for transferring authority and sharing legitimacy.

For Juárez, Hamnett interprets the choice to seek re-election as an attempt to continue work interrupted by the Reform War and French Intervention and to ground a new legitimacy in constitutional legality. At the same time, the chapter keeps opposition criticism visible: "re-electionism" began as a González Ortega camp attack on the 1865 extension and later entered porfirista and lerdista Liberal political vocabulary. The page ends as Hamnett turns to La Noria and the repression of rebels.
