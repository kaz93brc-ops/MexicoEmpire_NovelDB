## Summary

Hamnett presents Juárez's church policy after the imperial collapse as a practical modus vivendi rather than a simple anti-clerical line: clergy could be treated as citizens in electoral law, the 1867 convocatoria reaffirmed this, the government avoided a confessional stance, and worship freedom was maximized. The page also notes amnesty enabling Archbishop Labastida's return and Pius IX's late-June 1868 nomination of six bishops for vacant Mexican sees.

p.206 then shifts to Juárez's governing philosophy. Hamnett says Juárez valued the rule of law but governed a society where law was often subordinated to personal, factional, or corporate interests, and where the national government lacked the fiscal and administrative infrastructure to enforce law mechanically. Republican authority therefore had to be translated into practice through persuasion, maneuver, pressure, and force.

p.207 develops the political consequence: Juárez did not see the end of the Intervention as the end of his task. For Hamnett, 1867 was the beginning of national politics, constitutional consolidation, and the attempt to turn the Liberal Party into a responsible governing party; this conditioned Juárez's 1867 and 1871 re-election choices. At the same time, Juárez was losing some popular-level support while Porfirio Díaz cast himself as the voice of popular Liberalism.

Hamnett reads the 1867 convocatoria as a response to the porfirista challenge through direct appeal to the electorate. Juárez returned extraordinary powers at the opening of the new Congress on 1867-12-08, effectively accepted defeat on the direct appeal or plebiscite issue, and then shifted toward submitting the five August 1867 reform proposals as separate bills.

The page closes by naming Díaz as Juárez's chief opponent from the fall of the Empire to Juárez's death. Around Díaz gathered dissatisfied Liberal military heroes, radicals, constitutional purists, and figures such as Ignacio Ramírez and Ignacio Altamirano; Díaz's break with Juárez after the Liberal recovery of Mexico City drew on the González Ortega issue, his Puebla victory, and grievance over Juárez's treatment of Díaz and his brother.

## Creative Use

### Historical Use

- Show Juárez as a ruler facing re-election criticism, weak executive power, church settlement, army reduction, and Díaz's rise after victory.
- Place Díaz as a Liberal military hero and popular-Liberal spokesman before later dictatorship defines him.
- Treat church relations as political negotiation over citizenship, amnesty, bishops, and worship freedom.
- Use 1867-12-08 as a symbolic return from emergency rule to constitutional politics.
- Use the plebiscite defeat as a scene of Juárez's limits before Congress and party opposition.

### Creative Inference

- Juárez telling allies that winning the war began, rather than completed, national reconstruction.
- Radicals accusing Juárez of compromise over clergy political participation.
- Díaz arguing that Puebla repaid his debt to Juárez.
- Ramírez or Altamirano finding a guardian of 1857 principles in Díaz rather than Juárez.
- Juárez after returning extraordinary powers sensing the gap between legal ideal and political machinery.
