## Summary

p.226 resolves the previous page's unfinished "in" by comparing Oaxaca's 1871 refusal to recognize the federal government with Díaz Ordaz's late-1857 opposition to the coup of Tacubaya. Hamnett then summarizes the Plan of La Noria as a challenge to Juárez's re-election, the juarista capture of Congress and the Supreme Court, federal military intervention in the states, and violations of state sovereignty. The Plan cast the army as an agent for restoring constitutional legality and claimed that Juárez had betrayed Liberal achievements from Ayutla through the 1867 surrender of Mexico City.

Hamnett separates the Plan's rhetoric from his own evaluation. He treats many of its accusations as crude and not deeply considered, and reads the absence of a coherent political philosophy as evidence of the rebellion's personal character. Díaz nevertheless invoked the Constitution of 1857, electoral freedom, a state-delegate convention for an interim president after victory, direct presidential election, municipal autonomy, and repeal of oppressive taxation.

p.226-p.227 then connects La Noria to northern rebellions in Nuevo León, Zacatecas, and Durango from February 1871. Hamnett says these movements were politically linked but not militarily unified, allowing Juárez to rely on loyal governors and commanders such as Escobedo and Rocha and deal with them separately. Díaz tried to connect Oaxaca to the north through Manuel González, who contacted northern generals and leaders in the Puebla sierra. González is presented as a former Conservative who became personally loyal to Díaz and as a source of information from inside the National Palace.

The spread also records military preparation and failed openings: Hacienda of La Noria and the Dominican Convent of Santo Domingo in Oaxaca became arsenals, Díaz allies urged a rapid attack on Juárez, Rocha suppressed the Tampico garrison uprising in May-June 1871, and a Mexico City Citadel uprising failed without support. These failures weakened the prospect of a southern Díaz victory.

On p.227, Gerónimo Treviño's 29 September 1871 rebellion in Nuevo León becomes a major pressure on Juárez. Treviño denounced corruption and betrayal of 1867 expectations, claimed restoration of constitutional legality, styled himself commander of the Army of the North, and asserted Nuevo León's rupture with the federal government and recovery of state sovereignty. Juárez relied on Corona in Guadalajara to contain García de la Cadena in Zacatecas; Corona warned that Donato Guerra had joined Cadena and had links to Durango opposition, Sinaloa, and Mazatlán merchants. Hamnett reads the 1871 porfirista movement as drawing heavily from old González Ortega networks, and compares 1871 anti-Juárez criticism with constitutional and suffrage criticisms voiced since 1865.

- A Maximilian-centered ending in 1867 can make republican victory look final; this spread shows the restored Republic moving quickly into anti-re-election, state-sovereignty, and Liberal military faction conflicts.
- Porfirio Díaz's anti-French prestige becomes a political and military resource against Juárez rather than only republican merit.
