## Summary

Hamnett characterizes Juárez's emergency legislation as a legal basis for uncompromising resistance rather than as routine terror. The measures warned occupied towns, officials, and collaborators that acceptance of foreign-backed authority could be treated as treason, and they linked defense of the Republic, the 1857 Constitution, and the Reform Laws to severe penalties.

The spread then applies this policy to northern landholding politics. The 1863 San Luis Potosí decree and Juárez's March 1865 Chihuahua explanation extended the logic to officeholders under the Empire, while Juárez's letter to Escobedo singled out the Sánchez Navarro family of Coahuila for confiscation because of imperial collaboration.

Hamnett distinguishes this from simple anti-latifundio politics. Sánchez Navarro property and imperial-court ties made the family a political enemy, but Juárez cooperated with the large landowner Luis Terrazas in Chihuahua, so the conflict must be read through wartime allegiance and state authority.

On p.177 Hamnett shifts to Bazaine. Bazaine disliked Mexican Conservatives and Miramón, slowed Maximilian's independent imperial army, and pursued a political-military strategy aimed at depriving Juárez of moderate support behind the lines. Juárez despised Napoleon III and Maximilian but still treated Bazaine as a serious challenge to republican survival.

Maximilian's hope of drawing Juárez into a moderate liberal imperial cabinet is recorded as an imperial political misreading. Juárez's full rejection rationale continues onto the next page and is therefore held as an open point rather than finalized in a separate Fact Card.

## Creative Use

### Historical Use

- Juárez frames the future Maximilian death demand as an extension of existing emergency laws.
- In Chihuahua, Juárez explains the legal reach of the 1862 law against imperial officeholders.
- Juárez's letter to Escobedo turns Sánchez Navarro confiscation into political punishment for imperial collaboration.
- Sánchez Navarro landholding and court participation make confiscation both legal and symbolic.
- Bazaine advances militarily while trying to win the political middle ground behind Juárez's lines.
- Maximilian imagines Juárez as a moderate imperial minister, while Juárez rejects the premise.

### Creative Inference

- Juárez's circle debates the tension between republican principle and coercive punishment.
- Sánchez Navarro relatives fear republican return because property, status, and political safety are all exposed.
- Bazaine calculates how to support Maximilian while preventing an independent imperial army from escaping French control.
- Maximilian or an adviser misreads Juárez's refusal as negotiable.
- Moderate liberals weigh imperial participation against the likelihood of being branded traitors.
- Juárez justifies harsh measures as national survival while allies recognize the moral strain.
