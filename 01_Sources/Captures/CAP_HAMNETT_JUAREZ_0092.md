## Summary

Hamnett presents compromise with Maximilian not as a personal negotiation question but as a sovereignty question. Juárez would have treated concession to the Empire as recognition of foreign-backed authority over Mexico's government form, while Hamnett interprets Juárez as carrying the Republic's continuity in his own office and movement.

The spread then shifts to strategy. Juárez observed French operational strength and geographic weakness, especially the difficulty of holding Chihuahua, Mazatlán, Coahuila, and Paso del Norte while supply and communication lines lengthened. The 1865 move to Paso del Norte is therefore recorded as government continuity and strategic endurance, not simple flight.

Hamnett also records the Empire's internal vulnerabilities as seen from Juárez's side: Maximilian's clergy and Conservative problems, Miramón and Márquez being kept away, and rumors of clerico-Conservative unrest. The same section complicates republican virtue by distinguishing Escobedo's summary treatment of French prisoners from Vicente Riva Palacio's more lenient conduct in Michoacán.

The final p.179 material frames French withdrawal as a hard version of Napoleon III's policy of Mexicanizing the war. Private understandings between French commanders and Díaz or other Mexican generals reduced attacks on departing French troops and left republican forces facing weaker imperial forces, a situation Hamnett compares to the last phase of the Reform War with Maximilian caught in the middle.
