## Summary

p.82 presents autumn 1857 Mexico as a national leadership crisis: Hamnett says political life could not function without reconstructed national Liberal leadership, while the country looked again like the centrifugal 1823-24 moment in which state sovereignty claims endangered national integration. Comonfort understood national leadership reconstruction as his task but failed; by early 1858 Zuloaga's military coup put Conservatives in Mexico City, which in turn drove state resistance around defense of the Constitution.

Hamnett then narrows to Comonfort's 20 October 1857 cabinet. It was mainly moderate Liberal; Juárez, as Interior minister and Supreme Court president with succession rights, stood out as relatively radical and strong within the cabinet. Juárez also secured Manuel Ruiz's entry as Minister of Justice, while Hamnett treats Payno in Finance as the only other minister with marked weight.

p.82-83 follows the move from extraordinary powers and army reduction to the Plan of Tacubaya coup. Comonfort sought powers to suspend guarantees, reform finance, and reform the army; after the Cuernavaca military revolt Congress moved toward granting extraordinary powers. Comonfort used them to attempt large reductions in National Guards and the regular army, but by 1 December 1857 he was pressing Congress to revise the Constitution. Liberal division over constitutional reform led to Comonfort's alliance with Zuloaga, congressional exclusion, rule by executive power, and cabinet arrests including Juárez.

p.83 separates Bulnes's later complicity claim from Hamnett's rejection of it. Bulnes argued Juárez silently shared responsibility by knowing Comonfort's intent and not stopping it; Hamnett says evidence for this is lacking. Hamnett instead emphasizes Juárez's Romero information channel, his concern over coup rumors, and the 17 December arrest as evidence Comonfort did not trust him in the plot.

After the army turned against Comonfort, Hamnett treats the episode as an example of the military threat to constitutional order that Juárez had long warned against. Juárez remained under arrest in Mexico City until Comonfort ordered his release on 11 January 1858. Juárez then fled via Querétaro to Guanajuato, where on 19 January he attempted to reconstruct the Liberal government. This produced two rival governments and the three-year Reform War. Hamnett closes by stressing Juárez's dependence on state governors at Guanajuato, Guadalajara, and Veracruz; freeing himself from their tutelage became as important as defeating the Mexico City Conservative regime, but victory depended on those governors' armed power. The final sentence continues to the next page.
