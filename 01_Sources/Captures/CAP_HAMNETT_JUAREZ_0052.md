## Summary

p.98 opens by carrying forward p.97's Puebla diocese cost levy. The punitive measure drew a strong protest from Puebla bishop Pelagio Antonio de Labastida, and Comonfort expelled him even though Hamnett does not present him as clearly identified with the rebellion at that point. Hamnett treats Rome exile as a turning point that made Labastida a forceful opponent of Reform, especially because the government lacked an official spokesman in Rome.

Puebla conflict continued through 1856. Miramón's seizure of Puebla forced renewed government operations, and after a forty-day siege the city fell again on 3 December. Hamnett reads the Puebla Conservative action as a preview of the December 1857 and January 1858 crisis, and as exposing the intolerant edge of the Liberal revolution while knocking it off course.

The new Lerdo Law section states that on 1856-02-18, the Constituent Congress opening day, the Comonfort government issued a decree read here as Acta de Jacala and made the Juárez Law nationally valid. Juárez had already returned to Oaxaca for his second governorship. In his absence, Comonfort's government revived 1833-34 Liberal legislation, removed the civil legal force of religious vows on 1856-04-26, later advanced Civil Registry formation through the Lafragua Law of 1857-06-27, and issued the Lerdo Law on 25 June, with 1856 inferred from context.

Hamnett presents both the Juárez Law and Lerdo Law as initially avoiding direct confrontation with the Church. He argues that many in the Comonfort administration hoped to bring the ecclesiastical hierarchy into the Reform process and include the Church in Liberal modernization. At the same time, the two laws were unstable compromises between radical objectives and Catholic sensibilities. Federal-level moderates remained strong in 1855-57, but state politics differed; the Lerdo Law was moderate in design yet produced unexpected consequences and intensified conflict at every level.

The Lerdo Law required ecclesiastical authorities to value property and move it into private ownership, giving existing tenants or occupants priority. Rent was to become interest on capital value, new owners would pay the Church body, and the state would receive procedural revenue. If transfer failed after three months, the property was to go to public auction.

Ecclesiastical authorities denounced the law as confiscatory. Michoacán bishop Clemente de Jesús Munguía argued on 26 July, probably 1856, that Church property existed independently of government will and derived from the Church's social existence rather than secular grant. He also saw the law as continuing Gómez Farías's January 1847 attempt to raise funds at Church expense. Hamnett says social and ethnic relations markedly deteriorated after the law.

Pius IX's 1856-12-15 Allocution condemned the Juárez Law, Lerdo Law, Labastida's banishment, sequestration of Puebla diocesan property, removal of clergy voting rights in national elections, draft constitutional articles judged anti-religious, and civil authorities' disregard of the Holy See and ecclesiastical authorities. Hamnett then judges the government requirement that all public office-holders swear to uphold the Constitution as a major blunder. The archbishop condemned it and on 1857-03-15 declared that confessors would ask penitents whether they had taken the oath; the sentence continues beyond p.99.

## Shawcross Cross-Check

- Maximilian-centered narration can compress Reform Laws into Church property confiscation or anti-Church policy; Hamnett frames the Juárez Law and Lerdo Law as initially moderate reforms trying to avoid direct confrontation.
- The moderate design still produced conflict escalation and was received by Church actors as confiscation and religious attack.
- Labastida is not treated as always already a partisan Conservative politician; expulsion and Rome are framed as turning points in his anti-Reform role.
- Miramón appears in Puebla as a Conservative armed-political actor before the imperial period.
- Pius IX, the Holy See, and the archbishop connect Mexican Church-state conflict to international Catholic authority.
- The Liberal / Conservative divide must sit beside federal moderation, state radicalization, internal Church variation, and worsened social and ethnic relations.
