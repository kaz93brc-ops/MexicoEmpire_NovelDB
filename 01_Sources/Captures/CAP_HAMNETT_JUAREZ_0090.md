## Summary

Hamnett first closes the previous discussion by identifying [[Jose_Fernando_Ramirez|José Fernando Ramírez]] and [[Manuel_Orozco_y_Berra|Manuel Orozco y Berra]] as intellectuals whose participation helped expose divisions inside the Empire rather than secure consensus.

The page then shows Maximilian distancing the Empire from Conservative military leaders by sending [[Miguel_Miramon|Miramón]] to [[Berlin]] and [[Leonardo_Marquez|Márquez]] to [[Constantinople]]. Hamnett links this exclusion, despite Belgian and Austrian troops arriving, to deeper dependence on the [[French_Army|French army]].

Hamnett also presents a Church-state conflict: the papal nuncio arrived with papal dissatisfaction, Church-side demands for restoration collided with Napoleon III and Maximilian's aims, and Maximilian's decrees protected purchasers of [[Bienes_Nacionales|bienes nacionales]] while reopening sales under conditions.

The spread then enlarges the frame from Mexican imperial politics to European history. Hamnett argues that historians have underrecognized the intervention's European significance, treats Mexico as a priority of French policy for a time, and connects Juárez's resistance to French military humiliation and damage to the Napoleonic system.

The new section begins by showing Juárez's uncompromising resistance in legal form: extraordinary faculties, Veracruz closure, treason definitions, outlawing of intervention forces, death penalty for collaboration, and later treason penalties. These are stored as sovereignty-defense measures with cautions about republican coercion.
