## Summary

p.154 resolves the previous page's Seward sentence: the United States wanted to avoid complications with European powers, especially France. Hamnett treats this as limiting Romero's hope for collective action by American republics and as part of a wider pattern in which Juárez's direct appeal to Lincoln brought little practical gain.

Hamnett then links U.S. caution in 1861 to European debt claims, the outbreak of the American Civil War, Juárez's 17 July 1861 debt-payment suspension, the 31 October 1861 London Convention, and the failed Wyke-Zamacona Treaty of 21 November 1861. Mexican congressional rejection of that treaty left Juárez's government politically isolated and ended U.S. loan discussions.

p.155 shows Romero's disappointment with Seward and his suspicion that any U.S. loan would serve U.S. interests. The United States refused to join the tripartite debt collection while still recognizing European rights to collect debts and wage war, rejecting European territorial acquisition, and reserving naval action to protect U.S. interests in the Gulf.

The page then shifts to Mexico's position between European forces at the Gulf and the Confederacy on the northern frontier. It introduces early 1862 consideration of Corwin's 10 million dollar loan proposal, its mortgage on north-western public assets, a joint U.S.-Mexican commission, and Senate opposition because the loan could pull the United States into hostilities against European powers.

p.155 closes with Confederate diplomacy intensifying in 1862 and early 1863, the 5 May 1862 Puebla victory halting the intervention advance for about a year, and Juárez's government trying to resume arms purchases in the United States before the unfinished Lincoln export-ban sentence.

## Shawcross Cross-Check

- Maximilian-centered narratives often stress later U.S. pressure on France; this page corrects that by showing early U.S. reluctance to aid Juárez directly.
- Juárez and Romero should not be written as simply pro-U.S.; they hoped for aid while seeing Seward's coldness, dangerous loan conditions, and U.S. self-interest.
- European intervention here comes from debt, Congress, finance, and U.S. Civil War constraints, not only from French imperial ambition.
- Corwin's proposal connects to McLane-Ocampo Treaty as another sovereignty-risk bargain involving north-western assets rather than simple republican support.
- Confederate diplomacy connects pre-Empire border politics to later imperial and conservative strategies.
- Puebla's victory functions here as diplomatic and arms-procurement respite, not only as battlefield symbolism.
