## Summary

pp.136-137 present Chihuahua as a refuge that Juárez did not fully control. Creel's p.135-p.136 assessment praises Juárez's resilience, but Hamnett immediately emphasizes that Chihuahua state politics were not favourable to him.

Luis Terrazas appears as an anticlerical Liberal and vigorous enforcer of the Reform Laws, yet also as a regional power holder who used proceeds from nationalized ecclesiastical property and federal revenues for state needs. Hamnett frames Juárez's suspicion of Terrazas, and his later actions, as partly misjudged.

The Terrazas family background matters: municipal office, commerce, land, livestock, militia command, and a support base in the state legislature made Terrazas stronger in Chihuahua than Juárez. Hamnett also notes family ties and divided Liberal/Conservative alignments, which keeps Terrazas from fitting a simple ideological category.

p.137 traces escalation: federal instructions and the 1862 Finance Ministry circular were ignored, northern customs revenues slipped from federal control, Juárez placed Chihuahua under siege on 6 April 1864, the local Congress refused to publish the decree, and Patoni was used to enforce Terrazas's removal. Juárez's alternatives were weak: Casavantes lacked a state base, Trías was old and ill, and Terrazas still controlled the legislature.

Terrazas's land-grant politics connected ecclesiastical lands, unoccupied lands, and Juárez's own San Luis Potosí decree of 22 July 1863. Juárez sought elections in April 1865, but Terrazas won in June, showing the limits of central wartime control in Chihuahua.
