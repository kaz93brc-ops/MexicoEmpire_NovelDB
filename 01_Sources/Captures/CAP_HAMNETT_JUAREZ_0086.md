## Summary

Hamnett opens Chapter 8 by placing Juárez's European policy within a republican worldview: European monarchies appear as a standing threat to American republics, while the United States appears as a hoped-for natural ally whose actual conduct was inconsistent.

The chapter opening also shows Juárez reading European conflicts strategically. In May 1859 he treated war in Italy as a diversion of great-power attention, and in August 1866 he welcomed Prussia-Austria news as pressure on Napoleon III.

The section on debt then moves from long-term external debt to the London Convention. Hamnett traces the debt question to the 1824 London loans, later default and lost credit, 1851 restructuring, escalating total debt, Veracruz customs assignments, Ocampo's warning about Church-property expectations, failed U.S. borrowing, Lerdo's suspension proposal, and the still-heavy 1862 debt burden.

## Creative Use

### Historical Use

- Veracruz-based Juárez reads Italian-war news as a tactical opening while his government remains financially exposed.
- In 1866 Juárez's camp receives Prussia-Austria war news as a sign that Napoleon III's room for action is narrowing.
- A fiscal council scene can show Veracruz customs revenue already committed to creditors before policy choices are debated.
- Ocampo can puncture Liberal optimism that Church property alone will rescue the treasury.
- Spain, Great Britain, and France can share a debt-enforcement label while moving from different interests.

### Creative Inference

- Juárez may privately hope Bismarck-era conflict weakens Napoleon III, but such interiority must be marked as fiction.
- Liberal ministers may fall silent between republican principle and bankruptcy.
- Foreign creditors or Veracruz customs officials can view the Republic through credit risk rather than ideology.
- Imperialist characters can call suspension a violation of order while republicans answer with state survival.
