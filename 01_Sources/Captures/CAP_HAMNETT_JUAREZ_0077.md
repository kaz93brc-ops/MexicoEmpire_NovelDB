## Summary

p.148 completes the immediate transit-rights sequence: Lerdo de Tejada refused concessions, Comonfort later nullified the Sloo contract, and the United States first recognized Zuloaga because the Conservatives held Mexico City. Forsyth kept pressing for transit and border concessions, but the relationship broke over the one percent property tax and he left Mexico City on 1858-10-20.

The new recognition section generalizes the issue: Hamnett treats U.S. recognition or non-recognition as a long-running factor in Mexican politics. In 1858 no major power recognized Juárez's mobile government. Mata's April 1858 mission failed to obtain recognition or a loan, despite authority to negotiate against ecclesiastical property that the Liberals planned to nationalize.

p.149 shows the danger inside the opportunity. Juárez needed U.S. recognition for Veracruz security and to counter Spanish or broader European support for Conservatives, but Buchanan's government also saw Mexican weakness as a chance to secure concessions and transit rights. Hamnett contrasts Conservative fear of the United States as a threat to Mexican survival with Juárez's view of U.S. power as reinforcement for the Liberal cause; this is Hamnett's interpretive contrast, not a simple pro-U.S. label.
