## Summary

p.96 first completes Mantecón's warning from p.95. Hamnett reads it as suggesting that pressure on the Church's corporate position could join ecclesiastical opposition with village or community opposition when communities perceived their own interests as threatened.

The new section, "The Juárez Law," places Juárez in the Ministry of Justice and Ecclesiastical Affairs from 1855-10-06 to 1855-12-09. Hamnett describes the Juárez Law as an administrative justice law issued by Álvarez's executive power before the promised congress, aimed at limiting exemptions from civil law without immediately abolishing corporate privilege. Ecclesiastical and military courts remained for internal discipline but were removed from civil cases.

Hamnett separates Juárez's later memory of the law as Reform's starting point from the law's initial moderation. Juárez acknowledged help from Manuel Dublán and Ignacio Mariscal, but Hamnett treats the later self-image as partly retrospective and more radical than Juárez's 1855 position.

Episcopal responses varied. The Mexico archbishop condemned the law on 1855-11-28; Munguía of Michoacán challenged the government's authority over the ecclesiastical fuero and invoked the Holy See; Juárez answered from civil power and national sovereignty. Labastida protested on 1855-11-29 in less extreme terms, and Juárez replied on 1855-12-06 by insisting on obedience to the new law.

In Puebla, reaction outran episcopal control. The National Guard suppressed a city attempt on 1855-12-12, while Zacapoaxtla rebels led by an unnamed parish priest invoked religious defense and the 1836 Constitution. On 1856-01-23 the rebels took Puebla city after government troops defected. Haro, Osollo, and Miramón gathered around the revolt, which Hamnett treats as a serious blow to the Ayutla regime and to Comonfort's conciliatory middle course. Comonfort sent 15,000 federal troops, the Conservatives surrendered, Miramón hid for nine months, and the government overreached by charging suppression costs to the Puebla diocese.

## Shawcross Cross-Check

- Maximilian-centered narration can make Reforma and Church policy look like a simple attack on the Church; Hamnett frames the Juárez Law through jurisdiction, civil authority, and national sovereignty.
- Hamnett treats the 1855 Juárez Law as relatively moderate or conciliatory rather than as immediate total anti-Church policy.
- Catholic Church reaction is not singular: Mexico archbishop, Munguía, Labastida, Puebla local action, and Zacapoaxtla parish-priest leadership differ in intensity and outcome.
- Conservative action is not only reaction; religious defense, the 1836 Constitution, anti-Comonfort politics, and a provisional-president program appear together.
- Comonfort's conciliation was squeezed between armed Conservatives and radical Liberals backed by state governors' forces, a useful prehistory for civil war, intervention, and empire.
- Miramón and Labastida should be connected to the Maximilian period through this earlier Church-state and Conservative military context.
