## Summary

p.90 resolves the previous screenshot's unfinished Owen Chadwick point: Hamnett uses Chadwick and Fuentes Mares to present Reform liberalism as a moral doctrine, not just a program of political institutions. Fuentes Mares's image of Reform leaders as priest-like idealists is connected to Juárez's black clothing, but this remains a historiographical reading rather than proof of Juárez's personal intention.

Hamnett then separates the Conservative Party from the Catholic Church itself. He presents the party as a secular political grouping formed by Alamán in the late 1840s, while also saying Conservatives mobilized religious identity, popular piety, and religious feeling against the Liberal regime after Ayutla. Cuevas is used as a Conservative voice that framed Liberal reform as war on religion, the Catholic Church, Mexican national character, and national security. Hamnett's own explanation is more material and secular: Conservative religious critique served to gather beneficiaries of existing economic, social, and political structures.

Hamnett argues that Liberal anti-clericalism, especially radical state-level measures, was politically divisive and helped Conservative resistance before the December 1857 coup. Hale's comparative view is used to say that the Church question, more than abstract liberal philosophy, gave Mexican liberalism its distinctive conflict when compared with Argentina and Chile. Hamnett then broadens the problem from Mora to Cárdenas while keeping Cárdenas's exact identity for later confirmation.

Sierra's interpretation is then summarized: he labelled Ocampo's circle as Jacobins, saw them using revolution to remake social, governmental, economic, and legal structures, interpreted Comonfort as slowing that process, and placed Juárez under Ocampo's radicalizing influence. Sierra also interpreted the Juárez Law as a revolutionary act grounded in equality before the law. Hamnett immediately limits this reading by saying the evidence does not support treating Juárez at that point as a Jacobin.

On p.91, Hamnett stresses Juárez's relative moderation at least until 1858. Compared with Ocampo and Miguel Lerdo, Juárez treated church-state relations and Church property as legal problems more than confiscatory policy. His stance toward disamortization may have been conciliatory because of his Oaxaca background and knowledge of indigenous corporate institutions. During his 1847-1852 governorship he sought a working arrangement with the Oaxacan Church. During his 1856-1857 governorship he required clergy to obey federal and state law and not place corporate identity above civil law, but Hamnett distinguishes him from leaders who sought direct confrontation, persecution, or confiscation.

The new section begins by describing the institutional weakness of the Oaxaca diocese. It had lacked a bishop since 1827; Gregory XVI's 1831 appointment of six Mexican bishops did not include Oaxaca; in 1835 seven of ten Mexican sees were vacant; and a Primate was not established until 1839. The Mexican Church's leadership gap is linked to papal non-recognition of Mexican independence until 1838, but the explanation breaks off at the page end and must be completed with screenshot 49.

## Shawcross Cross-Check

- Maximilian-centered narration can make Reforma church policy look like simple anti-religion, but Hamnett frames it through corporate privilege, equality before the law, property, and legal obedience.
- Conservative Party is not Catholic Church itself; it is also a secular political force using religious feeling for mobilization.
- Juárez is presented as more moderate than Ocampo or Miguel Lerdo until at least 1858, and as reading the Church property issue through legal categories.
- Governor Juárez is not framed as pursuing confrontation or confiscation for its own sake; civil law over clergy is the key principle.
- Liberal internal difference matters: Ocampo / Lerdo radicalism and Juárez's legal-conciliatory posture should not collapse into one view.
- Conservative politics should not be reduced to faith alone; Hamnett links religious mobilization to defense of existing economic, social, and political structures.
- Oaxaca's long episcopal vacancy matters for local church-government relations.
- Papal recognition delay connects domestic church conflict to international Catholic politics and relations with Spain.
