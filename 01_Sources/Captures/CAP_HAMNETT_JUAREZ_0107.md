## Summary

p.208 completes the prior military-reduction sentence and shows Díaz opposing both the drastic reduction of the armed forces and the convocatoria. Hamnett then identifies a Díaz circle including Justo Benítez, Juan José Baz, Manuel Zamacona, Vicente Riva Palacio, and Luis Mier y Terán. He qualifies the rebellions against Juárez by arguing that they were not always initially porfirista, though many leaders eventually moved toward Díaz.

Hamnett interprets repeated rebellion as damaging Juárez's attempt to establish civil-law supremacy and as strengthening opposition claims that Juárez sought personal rule. He also presents provincial family, personal, and ideological factional struggles as becoming embedded in national politics. Puebla in 1869 and Zacatecas and San Luis Potosí in 1870 are linked to Congressional opposition, but Hamnett says no movement, even La Noria, became a coordinated national threat; Juárez could remove or neutralize unrest centers one after another.

The new section "LIBERAL DIVISIONS IN PUEBLA" begins on p.208. Puebla is dangerous because of its proximity to Mexico City. Méndez entered the sierra with about 7000 rifles and opposed the juarista governor Rafael García, whose local support was limited beyond Rafael Cravioto of Huauchinango.

p.209 moves into Puebla's military and fiscal pressures. Puebla city contained redundant officers expecting state pay. García tried to win over Lucas, and Juárez wrote Lucas on 19 February 1868 and again on 15 June to discourage subversion and armed revolt. The government feared Puebla sierra chieftains might unite with Miguel Negrete, identified by Hamnett as a former González Ortega supporter and long-standing Juárez enemy.

The page links restored Liberal reimposition of ecclesiastical disamortization to disturbance in Cuetzalan and treats Puebla sierra unrest as part of wider popular discontent reaching Tlaxcala and Hidalgo. Juárez appointed Ignacio Alatorre to command operations in Puebla rebel districts. Hamnett characterizes Alatorre's method as brinkmanship to avoid bloodshed. At Zacapoaxtla, Alatorre found deep hostility to García as an executive imposition against the Constitution, noted sierra links toward Veracruz and the Gulf coast, occupied three towns without fighting, and kept Lucas from joining Negrete's anti-Juárez effort.

## Creative Use

### Historical Use

- Juárez can be shown as governing after victory amid army reduction, redundant officers, provincial rebellion, state fiscal pressure, Church-property enforcement, and constitutional legitimacy disputes.
- Puebla sierra can serve as a setting where central authority does not automatically reach local commanders and communities.

- García's contested legitimacy shows that republican legality had competing local readings.

### Creative Inference

- Juárez recognizing that each neutralized rebellion exposes another weakness in state capacity.
- Puebla officers pressing the state treasury for pay while García lacks reliable support.
- Lucas reading Juárez's warning and weighing Negrete's opportunity against Alatorre's pressure.
- Alatorre in Zacapoaxtla sensing that anti-García anger is constitutional, local, and personal at once.
- Negrete trying to convert local discontent into an anti-Juárez coalition and failing to secure Lucas.
