## Summary

p.190 follows the defence and interrogation phase after the p.189 court-martial setup. The counsel were shocked that Juárez's government insisted on trying Maximilian, Miramón, and Mejía by military process under the 1862 law. Between 1867-05-24 and 1867-05-27 the prosecutor interrogated Maximilian, who refused substantive answers by denying the court martial's competence over political questions and argued that only Congress could try him, although Congress had not met since 1863. He also sought a personal interview with Juárez.

Miramón and Mejía used their replies to dispute republican legitimacy and responsibility. Miramón denied the Álvarez regime and the continued authority of the 1857 Constitution after Tacubaya, while Mejía contrasted his conduct with Escobedo's alleged prisoner executions. Miramón also said he had accepted the legitimacy of the junta of notables that invited Maximilian rather than endorsing the French Intervention itself. Hamnett treats these answers as deeply contrary to the Juárez government's current legitimacy claims.

On 1867-05-31 Escobedo rejected the argument that the 1862 law did not apply to Maximilian. With Díaz's safe conduct, the counsel left besieged Mexico City for Querétaro in company with Baron Anton von Magnus and M. Hooricks, whose spelling remains to be checked. After speaking with Escobedo, they met Maximilian on 1867-06-05 and learned that the government meant to allow only twenty-four hours for defence preparation before trial and execution.

p.190-191 then records the defence's jurisdictional and legal arguments. On 1867-06-06 José María Vázquez and Eulalio Ortega reiterated that a court martial was incompetent and invoked Jefferson Davis as a comparison. Riva Palacio criticized the 1862 law as contrary to the humanitarian principles of the 1857 Constitution, and the defence urged a public trial before normal federal courts. Hamnett says the counsel nevertheless understood the likelihood that the three prisoners would be made to die, and that the government remained determined.

Juárez granted only three additional preparation days, not the month the defence wanted. On 1867-06-08 the counsel reached San Luis Potosí and argued before ministers. Lerdo insisted after a three-hour discussion that the 1862 law governed the case. Juárez received Riva Palacio and Martínez de la Torre as old friends and denied personal enmity toward Maximilian, but he repeated the same legal position. The defence argued that the 1862 law belonged to confrontation, not reconciliation; Lerdo replied that Maximilian knew of the law when he entered Mexico. The defence further argued recognition by European Powers and the Brazilian Empire, Maximilian's lack of premeditated malice, his Mexicanizing efforts, and Article 23 of the 1857 Constitution. Ministers answered that the Constitution was suspended during war and that Juárez's extraordinary powers remained in force.
